# Writing Principles

Whereas spoken language emerges naturally within the hearing community (and sign language within the deaf), writing must be deliberately taught and learned. Unlike during a conversation, your readers are often unknown, invisible, and inscrutable. They cannot react, break in, or ask for clarification. As a result, writing is an act of pretence and an act of craftsmanship. Perfecting this craft is a lifelong calling, and mistakes are part of the game. However, the quest for improvement can be informed by a number of rules and principles. This handbook is designed to help you gain an understanding of these fundamentals and, in the process, improve your prose.

---

## 1. Read and Reverse Engineer

Good writers are avid readers. They have absorbed a vast inventory of words, idioms, constructions, tropes, and rhetorical tricks, and with them a sensitivity to how they mesh and how they clash. This is the elusive “ear” of a skilled writer—something everyone agrees cannot be explicitly taught.

Writers develop this writerly ear by spotting, savouring, and reverse-engineering examples of good prose. What are the signs of “good” prose? 

### 1.1 Signs of Good Prose

| Feature | Description |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Wording & Imagery** | An insistence on fresh wording and concrete imagery over familiar verbiage and abstract summary. |
| **Vantage Point** | An attention to the readers’ vantage point and the target of their gaze. |
| **Diction** | The judicious placement of an uncommon word or idiom against a backdrop of simple nouns and verbs. |
| **Syntax** | The use of parallel syntax. |
| **Surprise** | The occasional planned surprise. |
| **Detail** | The presentation of a telling detail that obviates an explicit pronouncement. |
| **Resonance** | The use of meter and sound that resonate with the meaning and mood. |

As you will expand your reading list, you will gradually begin to pick these things up and incorporate them into your own writing. So read more.

## 2. Follow the “Classic” Style

When composing a piece for a more generic readership, such as an essay, an article, a review, an editorial, a newsletter, or a blog, try to follow the “classic style.” This is one of the many styles a writer can use, but what sets the classic style apart is its guiding principle: seeing the world. In classic style, the writer can see something that the reader has not yet noticed, and they orient the reader’s gaze so that the reader can see it for themselves. 

Here, all eyes are on an agent: a protagonist, a mover and shaker, a driving force. The agent pushes or prods something, and it moves or changes. Or something interesting comes into view, and the reader examines it part by part. Classic style minimizes abstractions, which cannot be seen with the naked eye. This does not mean that it avoids abstract subject matter, only that it shows the events making up that subject matter transparently by narrating an unfolding plot with real characters doing things, rather than by naming an abstract concept that encapsulates those events in a single word.

In other words, present your concepts and ideas to the reader in a way that they can understand them without you holding their hands. This way, the reader will be able to connect the dots between lines and visualize even the most abstract concept you present to them.

---

## 3. Beware of the Curse of Knowledge

One of the main causes of incomprehensible prose is the difficulty of imagining what it is like for someone else not to know something that you know—the curse of knowledge. It simply does not occur to the writer that the reader does not know what the writer knows—that they have not mastered the patois of the writer’s guild, cannot divine the missing steps that seem too obvious to mention, and have no way to visualize a scene that to the writer is as clear as day. And so the writer does not bother to explain the jargon, or spell out the logic, or supply the necessary detail.

The best way to overcome this curse of knowledge is to show a draft of your writing to another person (not necessarily for editing), and often times you will see that what was clear and concise to you was not to another person.

---

## 4. Resist the Urge to Say Everything

Whereas those suffering the curse of knowledge do not provide enough information, leaving the prose full of jargon, there are also those who provide too much information that the reader cares or can care about at a time. This is particularly true for short pieces, such as blogs and op-eds. In such articles, the writer often tries to arrive at a single point of conclusion, and everything else facilitates this one point. But some writers feel the urge to say everything, or even the anxiety of missing out on something, and introduce multiple points of conclusion, each fighting for the reader’s attention. In the end, the reader is forced to read the prose by shutting down their brains.

There is only so much that can be said in a given text and only so much a reader can pay attention to at a time; keep this in mind and resist the urge to say everything you have to share at the same time.

## 5. Know Your Syntax

Understanding syntax—the linguistic study of the rules of how words combine to form phrases and phrases to sentences—can help a writer avoid ungrammatical, convoluted, and misleading prose. This is particularly useful when a sentence gets complicated, and we produce an error we would not otherwise produce. The knowledge of syntax allows a writer to reason their way to a grammatically consistent sentence when their intuitions fail them, and to diagnose the problem when they know something is wrong with the sentence but cannot put their finger on what it is.

The knowledge of syntax also allows the writer to judge the validity of a number of “rules” style guides prescribe. Take, for example, the notorious rule that you should not use the passive voice; use the active voice instead. This is a good rule in most situations; the active voice is more direct and concise. But there are some things that the passive voice can do better. For example, sometimes it just is not important or helpful to specify who performed the action you are talking about. Rewriting the sentence “This house was built in 1960” in the active voice would not only require you to dig up information you may not have, it would also bog down the sentence with an unnecessary detail. Does it really matter who built the house? Probably not.

Moreover, by using passive voice, you can uphold a very important rule: Put the emphatic words at the end of the sentence. If, for example, the done to of a sentence is the focus rather than the doer, then, unlike the active form, the passive form allows you to place it at the end of the sentence.

Syntax knowledge lets you practice yet another golden rule: Omit needless words.

In short, try and gain some understanding of syntax. This will allow you to not only identify problems at the sentence level but also let you fix them.

## 6. Maintain Coherence

A firm grasp of syntax can allow you to write grammatically correct sentences, one after another. But there needs to be a connection between them, what we call coherence. Without it, your writing will feel like ramblings—you are familiar with the words, but you have no idea what they mean in that order.

To write an intelligible text, you must first have a clear topic in mind and an outline that tracks the flow of your ideas. The topic corresponds to the small territory within the vast web of knowledge into which the incoming sentences should be merged; coherence begins with the writer and reader being clear about the topic itself.

Your outline does not have to be laid out in the traditional style, with letters, numbers, and roman numerals; all you need is something that establishes arcs of coherence—clear links from one sentence or passage to the next. You develop coherence by creating transitions between sentences or paragraphs that make it clear that they are connected. This is called coherence relation. 

### 6.1 Coherence Relations

| To Show... | Use Connecting Words Like... |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Similarity** | `similarly` or `likewise` |
| **Contrast** | `in contrast` or `on the other hand` |
| **Elaboration** | `moreover` or `in other words` |
| **Explanation** | `because` or `as` |

In a very basic outline, every paragraph has three parts: introduction, development, and conclusion. When you are writing something that has multiple paragraphs, those paragraphs are then divided into these three categories. Though this does not apply to every article, it is a good place to start. But whatever hierarchical organization you may follow, make sure your text as a whole is coherent.

Maintaining coherence in your writing will also allow you to remove needless or repetitive parts, which often is a major distraction to the reader. Repetition is easy to identify, but how do you omit irrelevant bits? Here you can try the Edgar-Allan-Poe method: If you can remove something from the text without hurting the point you are trying to make, you can safely remove it; every part must contribute to the narrative, and removing any of it should collapse the whole thing.

---

## 7. Revise, Revise, and Revise

Once you prepare the first draft and revise it, try to spend some time away from it. By the time you will come back to revise it again, more often than not, you will notice that you suddenly have quite some room for improvement that you previously did not realize. How long should you wait? The Roman poet Horace thought one should wait nine years, but that is a bit too much. A day—a few hours even—will work.
